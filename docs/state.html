BeSpoke Engine 0.4.0 · 21 November 2026

A game engine where the world keeps its own story

BeSpoke is a small, self-contained engine and writing language for story-driven games in which characters live their own lives, rules react to what happens, and puzzles can be solved in ways nobody scripted step by step. Every moment can be rewound and replayed exactly.

Written for two readers at once: anyone curious about what the engine does, in plain words, and development teams who want the detail underneath. Each feature card ends with an "Under the hood" line for the second reader.

In one minute

Most game engines are built around graphics and physics, and the story is a pile of small scripts attached to objects. BeSpoke turns that around. At its centre is a plain record of everything that is true in the game world, a clock that moves in counted steps, and characters who work through their own to-do lists. Pictures and sound only watch that world and show it.

That one choice buys a lot. Saving is exact. Rewinding is instant. A game behaves identically on a laptop, in a browser, or on a test server with no screen at all. A whole village of characters can get hungry, steal, gossip, and make arrests without anyone writing those events down, and a writer can still say exactly what must happen for the story to move on.

You write a game in a short, readable language (rooms, people, routines, conversations, rules), and the engine compiles it and runs it in a desktop window, a single web page, or a text-only test run.

The four ideas everything rests on

1. The world is a notebook of facts

Everything true is one short line: "Guard carries Cheese", "Bard charmed Guard, for the next 30 seconds". Nothing hides inside objects. Facts can expire on their own, so a distraction wears off without a timer to manage.

Under the hood: one contiguous arena of flat 4-tuples (subject, relation, object, expires_at_tick), no pointers inside, so a snapshot is a copy and a save is that copy with a CRC32.

2. Time moves in ticks

The game advances in counted steps, like a clock's second hand, at one per second by default (adjustable from ten a second to one every three seconds). Nothing depends on how fast your computer is, so the same button presses always produce the same game.

Under the hood: integer ticks only, no floating-point time in the simulation, seeded dice; the display redraws at 60 Hz independently.

3. Characters have to-do lists

Each character follows a routine ("walk to the cellar, wait, walk back") that can pause until something is true, be interrupted by something urgent, and resume where it left off. Writers describe behaviour in the order it happens.

Under the hood: cooperative fibers in a bytecode VM with priority interrupts and edge-triggered reactive rules; every room keeps simulating, watched or not.

4. Pictures and sound only watch

The window, the web page, and the speakers never decide anything. Each tick they read the world and draw or play it, which is why the same game runs anywhere, including with no screen for automated testing.

Under the hood: the VM owns main(), the clock, and the world ("Engine-as-Spine"); display and audio read frames and queued events through one-way bridges.

What it can do

A world that runs itself

Routines and interruptions

Characters walk their rounds, find their own way between spots and rooms, and drop what they are doing when something matters more.

Under the hood: waypoint graphs with A* pathfinding, wait until conditions, interrupt ... with: blocks.

Rules that notice

"When the cat holds the cheese, the guard turns to look." Rules fire the moment their facts become true together, and again when a fact fades.

Under the hood: when A and B:, when X forgets R:, and arrival rules for reaching a spot.

A village with its own life

Villagers get hungry and tired, work, trade, and steal; witnesses spread rumours; the law issues a warrant and makes an arrest. None of it is scripted event by event.

Under the hood: data-declared needs, roles, witnesses, rumours, and justice in society blocks, all stored as facts.

Characters with goals

A character can decide to pursue someone, cut them off, and confront them, then stand down once the matter is settled.

Under the hood: agenda declarations with priority, cooldown, and requires/unless triggers; interception fires a cue.

Fire, smoke, and water

A room can be laid out as a grid where fire spreads to flammable cells, smoke drifts, and water flows and puts fires out.

Under the hood: per-room grid overlays with burn, spread, dissipate, and flow rates; fire lifetimes are fact expiries.

Things that wear out

A cup of acidic grog eats through its cup in stages; pour it into the next one in time, and spill a little on every step you take.

Under the hood: container profiles with damage stages, exposure deadlines, spill rates, and failure outcomes.

Watch another room

The player can look into a room they are not in and watch what happens there, live, while their own room carries on.

Under the hood: DisplayBridge.capture_scene draws any room without moving the simulation's focus.

Characters who know things

Belief, doubt, and rejection

A character can know a fact, believe it, or reject it, and the story can branch on each. What someone knows is part of the world, not a flag in a script.

Under the hood: Knows, Believes, and Rejects facts, with rejection overriding the others.

Conversations that adapt

Dialogue options appear only when they make sense ("Show the ticket" only if you carry one). Answer by clicking, by a number key, or straight after typing "talk to the pilot".

Under the hood: choice "..." if <condition>: blocks; an open choice set is world state, so saving or rewinding mid-conversation restores it.

Present the evidence

Show a clue to a suspect. What they reveal depends on what they already know, and their composure can crack as the evidence piles up.

Under the hood: "present X to Y" records one fact per thing shown and fires a cue the script answers.

Characters who decide

A character weighs its options using only what it privately knows and picks one. In the card-game demo, each seat plays from its own hand and reacts to what the others reveal.

Under the hood: decision blocks score options with picks/drops lines; deterministic, with ties to the first option.

Duels of wit

Win an argument with the right comeback, learned by hearing it used against you. Opponents tire of being asked the same question.

Under the hood: data-declared challenge and reply pairs, opponent tiers, advantage, and staged mood decay.

Characters with values

Values the author defines

Give characters values such as mercy, justice, or loyalty on a scale from -10 to +10. The author names the values and what failing them looks like; the engine names none.

Under the hood: axis Mercy between Vengeance and "Condoning Harm" and remember Mercy(Valerius, 3); out-of-range numbers are compile errors.

Red lines

Some things a character will never do, however well the option scores. A captain who will not hang a child, or take a bribe, simply does not.

Under the hood: red lines Valerius: Hang, TakeTheCoin removes those options before any decision is scored.

Dilemmas with five answers

A character weighs one value against another, nudged by what is true in the world, and answers strongly or moderately either way, or asks for more to go on. Every answer has its own authored line.

Under the hood: dilemma blocks with weighs Justice against Mercy, leans lines, and five tiers whose boundaries a tiers 8 and 4 line can move; a missing tier line is a compile error.

Persuasion as a puzzle

Learn what someone values by watching and asking, then appeal to it. An appeal to a value you have not learned goes nowhere.

Under the hood: appeals 4 to Mercy lines and the typed appeal to mercy; learned values also feed the hints.

Characters who change

What the player brings about shifts a character's values over a long game, slowly and within limits, so a merciful winter can make a kinder spring.

Under the hood: drifts 5 toward Mercy when Repaid(Tam); whole-number points stored as facts, so saves and rewinds need nothing extra; an axis's drift step and a dilemma's drift cap set how fast and how far.

Puzzle and mystery tools

Hints that know what you know

Declare what the player must achieve, and the engine works out the nearest step they are missing. Hints come from inside the story while the character could reason it out, and from the author's side only when the player is truly stuck.

Under the hood: goal and need blocks with prerequisites and optional authored text per tier; three hint tiers generated when none is written, and as many more as the author writes.

Enough evidence, not all of it

An investigation can move on when the player has found two of five clues, or enough weight of evidence, rather than every single one.

Under the hood: 2 of Clue(_) and 6 points of Clue(_) in any condition.

Several ways to solve it

One puzzle, three approaches (by wits, by force, with a team). Choosing a path closes the others.

Under the hood: path blocks own their facts and routines; a locked route reroutes walkers.

Words of power

Spells built from discovered words (a power, an element, a form). Unlearned words do nothing; wrong combinations fizzle.

Under the hood: one spellbook per script; casting by name or by word triple, gated by what the caster knows.

Magic played as music

Play a four-note tune on a staff to cast a spell; play it backwards for the opposite effect.

Under the hood: draft declarations and weave handlers; the note staff is a built-in heads-up display.

Typed commands and clicks

Play by pointing and clicking with verbs, by tapping on a phone, or by typing "take the slip" or "use match on lantern". Scripts add their own phrasings and synonyms.

Under the hood: built-in command frames plus command and alias lines; nouns in scope only; tab completion and history.

Time, saves, and "what if"

Rewind anything

Rewind to the start or scrub backward and forward through recent history like a video. The world replays exactly, people, routines, and dice included, unless the game asks for fresh rolls.

Under the hood: each snapshot is a save held in memory; rewinds reroll keeps the dice going.

Safe saves

Save slots with a picture of the moment, quick save and load, and automatic recovery from a damaged save file.

Under the hood: checksummed .sav files with a .bak fallback; older save versions still load.

Borrowed time

Run a temporary copy of the world on a countdown. Close the loop in time and it folds back into reality; fail and it collapses, and you rewind.

Under the hood: copy-on-write branch worlds with tick budgets, goal checking, and atomic snapshots of the whole timeline.

Three eras at once

Characters in three different centuries pass objects to each other through a shared well, and a change in the past ripples forward.

Under the hood: forward-only causality between eras; a rewind restores every era together.

Sound and music, built in

A synthesizer, not sound files

Retro-style music and effects are generated as the game plays, on the desktop and in the browser, with no audio files to ship.

Under the hood: four-channel procedural synth; desktop through SDL2 audio, browser through Web Audio.

Music written in the script

Write melodies, bass lines, chords, and drums as short lines of notes. Each scene has its own music, and an alarm can take over until it passes.

Under the hood: score blocks in MML with loops, volumes, and envelopes; play score and stop score.

Runs anywhere it is pointed

Desktop window

A resizable window with verbs, an inventory, speech, a timeline, save slots, and a state inspector.

Under the hood: SDL2, a fixed 1280x720 frame letterboxed to any window size.

One web page

The same game in a single HTML file that works offline and on a phone, with touch-sized controls.

Under the hood: a freestanding WebAssembly build (about 690 KB with all fourteen browser demos) embedded in the page.

No screen at all

Run a game as text to see exactly what happens, tick by tick. Ideal for testing and for tools.

Under the hood: bespoke run game.bespoke --headless --ticks N.

See inside the world

Pause and open the state inspector: a map of who relates to what, why each dialogue option is offered or hidden, and what is about to expire.

Under the hood: a fact graph with keyboard and mouse navigation and relation filters, in both window and browser.

The language, in a few lines

A game is a plain text file. This one is a complete, runnable conversation: the Pilot's options depend on whether the Hero carries a ticket, and the player can type "take ticket" to get one.

scene Quay:
  cast: Hero, Pilot
  props: Ticket
  marks: Steps, Pier

relation Carries(holder, item)
relation Paid(who)

routine Wait(Hero):
  wait 1 ticks

on cue TalksTo(Hero, Pilot):
  say Pilot: "Crossing tonight?"
  choice "Show the ticket." if Carries(Hero, Ticket):
    remember Paid(Hero)
    say Pilot: "Step aboard."
  choice "Not yet.":
    say Pilot: "Suit yourself."

on cue Takes(Hero, Ticket):
  remember Carries(Hero, Ticket)
  say Hero: "One ticket."

command Takes: "take <thing>"

And a puzzle rule, a timed fact, and an evidence threshold:

on cue Uses(Hero, Matches, Lantern):
  remember Lit(Lantern) for 30 ticks
  say Hero: "The wick takes."

when Rung(Bell) and Lit(Lantern):
  remember Signalled(Quay)

routine Inspector(Moss):
  wait until 2 of Clue(_)
  say Moss: "That is enough to go on."

The full reference is script_spec.md. Every example in the authoring guide is compiled and played by the test suite, so the guide cannot drift from the engine.

For development teams

How a game flows through the engine

Script.bespoke text: scenes, rules, routines, dialogue, scores
Compilerchecks names and types, points at mistakes with line, column, and a suggestion
Packagecompact .bspk bytecode, versioned, loadable without the source
VM and worldfibers, rules, and agents step the fact arena once per tick
Hostsdesktop window, web page, or headless runner read frames and send input

What a team gets

  • One command to start. bespoke init my_game writes a project with a starter story, tests, a desktop build, a single-file web build, and a CI workflow that builds and tests on Linux and macOS and smoke-tests the web version. That workflow has been run on GitHub and passes.
  • A toolchain that ships as one file. The static Linux download compiles, checks, and runs scripts and creates projects with nothing else installed. Building a game's own executable needs Zig 0.17.1 (and SDL2 for the window).
  • Tests that read like a story. A headless run prints what happens tick by tick; each demo's transcript is recorded and compared byte for byte, along with a pixel hash of every scene, so a behaviour change cannot slip through unnoticed.
  • A host API with a contract. Zig hosts drive the engine through a documented, supported surface (API.md); anything unlisted is internal and may change.
  • File formats that keep working. Packages and saves carry versions; the reader accepts package versions 1 to 36 and save versions 1 to 4, and files written by earlier releases are loaded on every test run (COMPATIBILITY.md).
  • Hardened input. The lexer, parser, code generator, package and save loaders, music parser, and command interpreter run under mutation tests, and an unsupported file version is refused with a named error before any game state changes.
  • Editor support. A VS Code syntax grammar generated from the compiler's own word list, so it cannot fall out of date.
  • A recorded decision trail. Over 140 numbered design decisions, each with its context, the alternatives rejected, and when to revisit it.

Built to stay small

  • Written in Zig, with no outside libraries in the compiler or the simulation. SDL2 is used only for the desktop window and sound; the browser build uses nothing.
  • The engine never names a demo: all demo content lives apart from the engine and reaches it only through the public module, and a check enforces that.
  • MIT licensed (with a voluntary note on AI-assisted use).

What it does not do yet

  • Pixel art is new. A game can carry its own pixel art (backdrops and sprite sheets, from PNG files or typed into the script), and the script picks what each thing shows from its facts, such as a lamp that lights, with parts drawn on top of their hosts. Older demos are still drawn by code.
  • Windows is deferred, and macOS is checked by automated builds only.
  • The language is still pre-1.0. It may change before 1.0; removals follow a published deprecation policy, and released packages and saves keep loading.
  • Small writing gaps: inside an event handler, a lowercase placeholder (such as victim) matches any character but is not yet usable in the handler's body; a chosen path in a multi-path puzzle cannot be switched; a choice offers at most nine options.
  • Deliberately out of scope: 3D, physics, and networked multiplayer. BeSpoke is for narrative and systemic games.

What is next

  • BeSpoke 1.0, when the maintainer decides the language and host API are ready to promise.
  • Windows, once a contributor who uses Windows can maintain it.

Get started

  1. Play. Open any page in the repository's site/ folder in a browser, or run a demo in a window with zig build run-proof -- --tavern.
  2. Download. The latest release has a static Linux command-line tool, an SDL2 desktop build, and the browser runtime.
  3. Make a game. Follow FIRST_GAME.md from bespoke init to a single HTML file you can upload anywhere. The tutorial is run by CI, so it works as written.
  4. Go deeper. The authoring guide, the architecture, and the source on GitHub.

Found something odd? Write down the demo and roughly when it happened. Every run plays the same way, so a problem you can describe is a problem that can be reproduced and fixed.

The Architectural Pillars

Simulation as First Principle

Drawing spiritual inspiration from the deterministic clockwork of Chris Sawyer's RollerCoaster Tycoon, the actor/cue architecture of Sierra's SCI and LucasArts' SCUMM, and the emergent relationship graphs of Dwarf Fortress. BeSpoke counts time in whole steps (ticks) and stores the world as facts, so the same inputs always produce the same story.

PILLAR 01

Engine-as-Spine

The engine runs the game; the screen only shows it. The engine owns the clock, the characters, the map, and the facts. The window (SDL2 on the desktop, a canvas in the browser) draws what the engine reports and passes player input back; it never changes the world itself.

// A host starts a script, then asks for ticks
_ = try host.start_script(&world, &spatial, &vm, &compiled);
_ = try host.tick(&vm, &compiled);
PILLAR 02

Flat Relational State

Everything the game knows is a row of four values: (subject, relation, object, expires_at_tick), such as "the Guard carries the Cheese". A fact can carry an expiry tick, so a charm, a distraction, or an alarm ends on its own without cleanup code.

remember Carries(Guard, Cheese)
remember CharmedBy(Guard, Bard) for 180 ticks
PILLAR 03

Fibers & Time Travel

Write behaviour like a play script: a routine walks, waits, and speaks, and pauses with wait or wait until without blocking anything else. The facts sit in four flat columns, so a snapshot copies those columns and the name tables as raw bytes; rewinding copies them back.

walk Guard to Cellar
wait 5 ticks
walk Guard to Hearth
Proofs of Concept

Classic Adventure & Systemic Proofs

Thirteen proofs of concept drive the engine end to end. Each runs without a window and checks its own outcome; twelve also rewind and check the world matches (Algiers does not yet). They live in proofs/, depend on the engine through its public module, and build as a separate bespoke-proofs binary; the engine itself never imports a proof. Eleven play in the viewport above. Every card opens the script its proof runs; the browser decks run those same scripts.

Proof 01 · Systemic emergence

The Tavern Heist

A patrolling Guard carries the Cheese. The Bard's lute charms him, and the Cat takes the cheese while he is distracted. No cutscene: rules react to facts as they change.

Proof 02 · Environmental hooks

Booty Island Spitting Contest

Guybrush needs a winning spit. He sounds the ship's horn to distract the crowd, moves the distance flags, drinks something thick and green, and waits for the wind.

Proof 03 · Multi-scene crafting

The Cat Moustache

Gabriel must pass as Mosely to rent a motorcycle. Tape, a marker, syrup, a passport, and some cat hair combine across three scenes into a convincing disguise.

Proof 04 · Emergent society

The Living Village

Six villagers with hunger, energy, and money run their own days. A starving drifter steals, a witness talks, rumours spread, and the Sheriff makes an arrest, with nothing scripted in between.

Proof 05 · Arrival rules

The Lochmarne Goat

A tethered goat knocks George down whenever he heads for the cellar. Move the plough, tangle the goat's rope around it, and the way down to the crypt is clear. Each step is an arrival rule in the script.

Proof 06 · Three eras, one world

Day of the Tentacle Trio

Three characters in three eras pass items through time. A change in the past is a fact the future reads, so each era's puzzles depend on the others.

Proof 07 · Container degradation

Caustic Grog

Three cups of grog eat through themselves on the way from the barrel to the lock. Pour from cup to cup before each fails, and arrive with enough to dissolve the lock.

Proof 08 · Learning dialogue

Insult Sword Fighting

Every duel teaches insults and their comebacks. Opponents grow tired of repeated questions, and the duel's rounds and moods live in World facts, so a rewind replays them exactly.

Proof 09 · Stageplay paths

Algiers: Three Paths

Wits, Fists, or Team: choosing an approach locks the other routes, limits the dialogue to that approach, and runs only that approach's solution.

Proof 10 · Branch worlds

The Middlesex Anomaly

The world splits into a temporary copy with a countdown. Carry one object to the right place before time runs out and the copy merges back; miss it and the copy collapses, so you rewind and try again.

Proof 11 · Musical drafts

The Bell Tower

Play four-note tunes (drafts) on a musical staff to change the world. Played backwards, each tune does its opposite.

Proof 12 · Fire, smoke, and water

The Castle Fire Escape

Burn a tapestry to find the hidden door, then soak the door before the fire reaches it. Water floods back through the corridor and puts the fire out.

Desktop only: zig build run-proof -- --grid
Proof 13 · Agendas and typed commands

The Larkspur Observatory

Investigate a missing comet plate while the staff go about their night. Each of them has an agenda and intercepts you when your evidence threatens it. Type your commands.

Desktop only: zig build run-proof -- --observatory
Dialogue

Dialogue That Reads the World

Adventure-game dialogue usually tracks what the player has said with a pile of yes/no flags. In BeSpoke, each dialogue option carries a condition written against the world's facts, and the option appears only while that condition holds. Both examples below are excerpts from the Insult Sword Fighting proof's script.

Pattern 01 · Insult Sword Fighting

A Duel Declared as Data

The script lists insult and comeback pairs; the engine's duel module turns them into facts (HeardInsult, MasteredReply, Counters). Each round, the replies the player may choose are the ones they have learned, filtered through the same dialogue engine as every other choice. The dialogue text is shortened here. 

duel InsultSwordFighting:
  pair Insult1:
    challenge "..."
    reply "..."
  # sixteen pairs in all
  fallback "..."
  win 3
  relations HeardInsult MasteredReply Counters SwordMasterVariant IsSwordMaster Advantage DuelsWon
  fatigue Inquiries "welcoming":
    stage AnnoyedWith 2 100 "annoyed"
    stage FuriousWith 3 250 "furious"
Pattern 02 · Repetition & Fatigue

Characters Who Lose Patience

Ask the barkeep the same question twice and the answer changes. Each option checks a fact, and the annoyance is a fact that expires after 100 ticks, so the barkeep calms down on their own. 

on cue TalksTo(Guybrush, Barkeep):
  choice "Tell me about Governor Marley." if not Inquired(Guybrush, Marley, _):
    say Barkeep: "Governor Marley? Sharp as a cutlass and twice as quick."
    remember Inquired(Guybrush, Marley, 1)
  choice "Tell me more about Governor Marley." if Inquired(Guybrush, Marley, 1):
    say Barkeep: "Look, I already told you. Her mansion is on the hill."
    remember Inquired(Guybrush, Marley, 2)
    remember AnnoyedWith(Barkeep, Guybrush) for 100 ticks
  choice "About Governor Marley..." if AnnoyedWith(Barkeep, Guybrush):
    say Barkeep: "Are ye deaf, pirate? Buy a grog or get out of my tavern!"
    remember FuriousWith(Barkeep, Guybrush) for 250 ticks
Architecture

How the Engine Is Built

Three views of the same design: what owns the clock, how a player's action becomes a story beat, and how one script reaches every platform.

Engine-as-Spine

The engine is main(). A host only asks for the next tick; one discrete tick runs the VM, the rules, and the declared subsystems, all reading and writing one World of facts.

From Action to Story Beat

Nothing calls a cutscene. A cue changes facts, rules notice the combination on the next tick, and routines pause or resume around them. Speech and music are read from the result.

One Script, Every Platform

A game is a .bespoke script. The engine's compiler turns it into a checked .bspk package, which the same runtime plays in a desktop window, headless in tests, or in the browser.

Engine Architecture

Comparing Engine Philosophies

General-purpose engines are built around drawing frames, with game logic attached to the render loop. BeSpoke starts from the simulation and treats drawing as one consumer of it. The left column describes the common default, not every engine.

Dimension Visual-First Scene Graph Engine Simulation-First Relational Spine (BeSpoke)
Who owns main()? Graphics pipeline & frame-rate render loop The simulation, one whole tick at a time (1 per second by default; the display redraws at 60 Hz)
World State Representation Transform hierarchies, heap pointers & GC objects Rows of facts in one fixed block of memory
Time-Travel & Save States Custom serializers & object-graph reconstruction Copy the fact columns and name tables as raw bytes, with a checksum
Temporal Logic (Waiting, Routines) Delta-time accumulation, per-frame callbacks, state booleans Routines that pause with wait and resume on a later tick
Ephemeral State (Buffs, Alerts) Explicit timer handles & manual polling loops An expiry tick on the fact itself; the engine drops it when the clock passes
Toolchain & Footprint A large editor and runtime to install The Zig 0.13 toolchain; the core needs nothing else, and SDL2 only for the desktop window

"A simulation game is a living clock of emergent rules. The craft is building a runtime clean enough that the gears never grind."
